# Provision map

Where common lease provisions tend to sit, what to search for, and what usually turns out to be the real answer. Use it to start on an unfamiliar provision — not as a substitute for reading the document, since numbering varies by lease form and any of this can be relocated by an amendment.

## Orientation: the summary page

Many office lease forms open with a "Summary of Lease Provisions" grid: suite number, rentable area, pro rata share, commencement and expiration, base rent, monthly operating expense charge, base year, security deposit.

Read it first on every lease. It gives you the economics in one page and tells you which provisions are even live — a pro rata share of "NA" and an operating expense charge of "NA" means the expense article, if present at all, does not apply. The grid is also frequently what a later amendment edits, by reciting "Section 1(c) shall be amended as follows."

Treat it as an index, not as authority. Where the grid and the operative article disagree, the article governs, and the disagreement is worth reporting.

## CAM and expense recovery

**Typically:** an article titled "Operating Costs Adjustment," "Operating Expenses," or "Additional Rent," usually early — sections 2, 5 or 6.

**Search:** operating expense, operating cost, common area, additional rent, prorata, pro rata, proportionate share, base year, expense payment, common expenses, real estate taxes, CAM

**Structure to expect:** a definitions subsection (Tenant's Prorata Share, Real Estate Taxes, Operating Expenses, Utilities), then an adjustment mechanism (estimated monthly payments, year-end reconciliation, audit rights).

**Where the answer usually is:**

- The **exclusion list**, which is often longer than the inclusion list and holds the commercially significant limits: management fee caps, capital expenditure treatment, exclusion of leasing and ownership costs.
- Whether taxes and utilities sit **inside** the operating expense definition or are billed as **separate independently calculated buckets**. Both are common and they behave differently.
- **Gross-up**: to 95%, to 100%, only for occupancy-variable costs, or expressly prohibited.
- Whether it is a **base year stop** (tenant pays only its share of increases) or **full net** (tenant pays its share of the whole amount). A base year appearing in the summary grid does not settle this — check the adjustment mechanism, since some forms use the base year only to mark when estimated billing starts.

## Utilities and submetering

**Typically:** its own short article near services, or folded into the expense definitions.

**Search:** utilities, submeter, sub meter, separately metered, electricity, excess utilities, after hours, HVAC

Watch for after-hours HVAC billed as a separate charge, and for a submetering clause that overrides the general expense treatment for one tenant.

## Chilled water and district energy

**Typically:** a standalone amendment rather than the base lease, since these arrangements are usually retrofits.

**Search:** chilled water, chill water, CHW, condenser, tons, ton-hour, contract demand, district energy, delta T

**Structure to expect:** definitions (Contract Demand, Actual Demand, Consumption, Ton), a service section, a rates section that points to an exhibit, and exhibits carrying the demand schedule and the rate table. The rates are almost always in the exhibit, not the body.

**Where the answer usually is:** the charge decomposes into commodity, consumption and demand components with different escalation mechanics, plus penalties for failing a return-temperature target. Note whether the demand charge is billed on contract or actual demand, whether it survives service suspension, and whether the contract demand ratchets up when actual demand exceeds it.

Also check whether a chilled water amendment lets the tenant hand back rooftop condenser space — the give-back is often in a different, earlier amendment.

## Rent and escalation

**Typically:** section 2 or 5, plus a rent schedule exhibit.

**Search:** base rent, monthly rent, escalation, increase by, anniversary, CPI, Consumer Price Index, rent commencement

Fixed percentage bumps (commonly 3% annually) are most usual; CPI adjustments appear in older leases and usually carry a floor and a cap. Amendments frequently reset base rent outright, so the base lease schedule is often stale.

## Term, renewal and extension

**Typically:** section 1 or 2 for the initial term; renewal options are often in an exhibit of special conditions or added by amendment.

**Search:** initial term, renewal term, extension term, option to renew, expiration, automatically renew, notice, exercise

**Where the answer usually is:** the notice window (frequently 90 or 180 days before expiration), whether exercise is irrevocable once given, whether the option survives a default, how many options remain unused, and what rent applies during the renewal. Auto-renewing annual terms are common for small storage and equipment spaces.

Amendments often replace the renewal structure wholesale, sometimes with an express clause superseding all prior extension rights.

## Termination, surrender and partial give-back

**Typically:** several places at once — a default article, a casualty and condemnation article, and any special termination rights added by amendment.

**Search:** terminate, termination, right to return, surrender, give back, default, cure period, restore, remove

**Where the answer usually is:** partial give-back rights for specific spaces (rooftop, generator, condenser, storage) usually sit in the amendment that added that space, not in the base lease, and typically carry a long notice period and a restoration obligation. Note what triggers the right and what condition the space must be returned in.

## Assignment and sublease

**Typically:** a mid-document article.

**Search:** assignment, sublease, sublet, transfer, consent, recapture, permitted transferee, change of control

**Where the answer usually is:** whether landlord consent may be withheld unreasonably, whether landlord may recapture instead of consenting, how excess rent from a sublease is split (halves are common), and whether affiliate transfers are carved out of the consent requirement.

## Maintenance and services

**Typically:** adjacent articles — one for landlord obligations, one for tenant.

**Search:** maintenance, repair, services, landlord shall keep, HVAC, janitorial, cleaning, common areas

**Where the answer usually is:** the boundary between landlord and tenant responsibility, and whether landlord's obligation runs to the whole building or only the common areas. This interacts directly with expense recovery — a service limited to common areas in the maintenance article is generally recoverable only to that extent, even if the expense definition lists it without qualification.

## Insurance and indemnity

**Typically:** a mid-to-late article.

**Search:** insurance, liability, indemnify, hold harmless, waiver of subrogation, certificate, additional insured

Note required limits, who must be named, and whether subrogation is waived mutually. Landlord's property insurance is usually a recoverable expense; tenant's own coverage is not.

## Holdover

**Typically:** a short article near the end.

**Search:** holdover, hold over, month to month, sufferance

Multipliers of 150% or 200% of the last rent are typical. Check whether additional rent is included in the multiplied amount and whether consequential damages are recoverable.

## Reading amendments

Amendments follow a predictable shape, and each part is useful:

- **Recitals** enumerate every prior amendment by date and subject. This is the cheapest way to build the chain and to detect documents you do not have.
- **Numbered sections** state what changes, usually by reciting the lease section being amended. A section not mentioned is unchanged.
- **A ratification clause** confirms everything else stays in force.
- **A conflict clause** stating the amendment controls over the lease. This is what makes the most recent amendment addressing a provision the operative one.

Three habits worth keeping:

- Read the recitals of the *latest* amendment first — it summarizes the whole history in one place.
- Check whether an amendment applies to all the tenant's premises or only some. Provisions scoped to particular suites are common and easy to miss because they turn on a single phrase.
- Search inside the other PDFs before concluding an amendment is missing. Amendments are routinely scanned into the back of the base lease file with no hint in the file name.

Relocation amendments deserve particular care. When an amendment moves a tenant from one suite to another and closes with "except as expressly modified above, the Lease remains unmodified," the new suite inherits the treatment the old one had — including any expense obligation or carve-out attached to it. That inheritance is rarely spelled out and is usually the answer to "does this space pay?"
